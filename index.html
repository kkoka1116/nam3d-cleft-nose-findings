Nasoalveolar molding (NAM) outcomes research

Measuring the cleft nose from clinic photos: what works today

Nasal symmetry is a key outcome of NAM, but it is usually judged from ordinary clinic photographs taken at different distances and angles. We asked whether new AI tools can turn those photos into accurate 3D noses, and how to measure reliably in the meantime.

1

Measure in 2D, on the right view. Nostril width from the frontal photo; nostril height and area from a steep view from below. That gave about 4–7% error in our tests.

2

Use full-resolution photos. Collage exports are too small: a perfectly symmetric nose can appear 13–36% asymmetric.

3

AI 3D is promising but not ready to measure. The best model (MoGe-2) recovers nose shape to about 1 mm, but its measurements did not beat careful 2D.

A clinic photo becoming a 3D surface (MoGe-2)

Why this matters

Photos can create change that isn't there

When we compare a baby's nose before and after NAM, we want differences to come from treatment, not from how the photo was taken. A slightly turned head, a lower camera, or a cropped image can shift a nostril ratio by more than the treatment effect.

3D geometry would remove that problem, because a 3D nose can be measured from any angle. The question was whether AI can recover reliable 3D from the photos clinics already have.

The measurements we focused on

  • Nostril width ratio: cleft side ÷ non-cleft side
  • Nostril height ratio: cleft ÷ non-cleft
  • Nostril area ratio: cleft ÷ non-cleft
  • Supporting: alar base position, columella deviation, nasal tip position

Ratios need no ruler or penny, so they suit retrospective photos without calibration.

What we did

You can only judge a measurement tool against a known truth, which real clinic photos don't provide. So we tested on faces whose exact 3D shape we know, then looked at real clinic photos.

Best use case today

A measurement protocol clinics can adopt now

The most useful result is not an AI model but a clear answer to "which photo should I measure each ratio on, and how good must the photo be?" Every number below comes from our tests, where the true answer was known.

1

Take two full-resolution photos

A straight frontal view with the camera level with the nose, and a steep view from below (head tilted back ~55°) with both nostrils fully open to the camera.

2

Measure each ratio on its best view

Width on the frontal photo. Height and area on the view from below. Avoid three-quarter and profile views for these ratios.

3

Check the photo is big enough

The face should span at least ~550 pixels, so each nostril is about 30 pixels across. Smaller images add error that no software can remove.

4

Use AI 3D as a visual aid

MoGe-2 surfaces help you see nasal shape and spot problem photos. Don't take millimetre readings from them yet.

Photo checklist

  • Send or keep the original camera files, not collage exports
  • Nose and nostrils free of tape and appliances when possible
  • Frontal: camera level with the nose, head not turned
  • From below: nostrils fully visible, a consistent tilt between visits
  • If using a coin for scale, hold it at the nostrils' depth, flat to the camera
  • Same views at every visit, so changes reflect treatment

Finding: photo size

Collage exports are too small to measure

In the collage sheets clinics send, the face is only 120–300 pixels wide, so a nostril is 7–17 pixels. At that size, a one-to-two pixel uncertainty when marking an edge produces 14–38% error in the width ratio, and a perfectly symmetric nose can appear 13–36% asymmetric. Requesting original full-resolution photos is the simplest, biggest improvement.

Finding: AI 3D reconstruction

One AI model rebuilds the nose far better than the rest

We measured how far each model's 3D nose surface was from the true shape, and how wrong the tilt of its surface was. MoGe-2 (Microsoft) was clearly best: about 1 mm of shape error and 9–14° of tilt error, roughly half that of Depth Anything 3, the model this project started with. Most models flattened or shifted the nose by several millimetres, a lot for a nostril about 5 mm across.

How far each model shifted the whole nose

Shift along the camera's line of sight. On the real cleft models MoGe-2 was within about 1 mm; Depth Pro was about 14 mm off because it misjudged the camera's lens.

Finding: 2D vs 3D measurement

For nostril ratios, careful 2D is as good as AI 3D

Measured on the right view, plain 2D matched or beat measurements taken from every AI model's 3D nose. MoGe-2's 3D tied 2D on the views from below, but on an angled (20°) photo it was the worst method tested. So AI 3D doesn't yet improve the main ratios.

Your clinic photos

Each photo becomes its own 3D surface

MoGe-2 rebuilt the part of the face each photo sees. Drag the slider to lift the photo into 3D, rotate with the mouse, and switch to clay shading to judge shape without skin colour. On views from below, nostril openings, the columella and the cleft are clearly modelled.

Try measuring on the surface

Choose a measurement and click its two endpoints on the 3D surface. Ratios (cleft ÷ non-cleft) are calculated automatically.

For exploration only. The AI's sense of size is unreliable on cropped clinic photos: it placed these faces 0.5–1.3 m from the camera. Millimetre values are illustrative, and even the ratios are not yet validated on real infants.

Why we don't combine the photos

Different moments, different faces

A full 3D face would need the photos to be combined. Our checks accepted 0 of 125 attempts. Even virtual photos taken at the same instant of a still face didn't line up well enough. Clinic photos add a bigger problem: they are taken seconds apart while the baby moves, cries and is re-taped, so the face itself changes between shots.

Next: the build in progress

Instant measured 3D with the iPhone's Face ID camera

AI can only guess depth from a flat photo. The iPhone's front TrueDepth camera, the one used for Face ID, measures it: it projects an invisible dot pattern and reads how the dots land on the face. Each depth frame takes about 1/30 of a second, fast enough to freeze a moving baby. We are building a small capture app that records a TrueDepth depth map and the matching front-camera photo together.

What a TrueDepth capture could look like

A simulated preview built from a real NIH cleft model's true surface, with assumed sensor behaviour: a depth sample about every 0.6 mm, 0.5 mm noise, and no reading where the surface turns steeply away. A single frame looks rough, so the app captures a 1-second burst: averaging ten frames gives a smooth surface from measured data alone. Fusing with MoGe-2 keeps TrueDepth's true shape and scale, borrows only MoGe-2's fine smooth detail, and fills the small gaps; filled areas are shown in blue because their shape is AI-estimated. Colour elsewhere shows shape error on the same scale as above.

The capture protocol we are building

1

Front burst

Phone ~30 cm from the face; one tap records ~1 second of paired depth and photo frames. Sound and vibration cues, since the screen faces the baby.

2

From-below burst

Head tilted back so both nostrils face the phone; another 1-second burst. The sharpest frame of each view is kept.

3

Measure in true 3D

Real millimetres and real head angle: angle-corrected nostril ratios, tip projection and columella shape, with no AI guessing of depth.

4

Validate, then use

Pilot on adult volunteers, then (with IRB approval) infants, compared with 2D protocol measurements and a clinical 3D camera.

Important: generic iPhone "face scan" features fit a smooth average adult face template, which would erase cleft anatomy. The app saves the camera's raw depth instead. Use on infants requires ethics approval; the projector is the same eye-safe system used by Face ID.

Summary

What is ready, and what isn't

Ready to use

  • Nostril width ratio on full-resolution frontal photos
  • Nostril height and area ratios on steep views from below
  • The photo checklist and minimum photo size

Promising, needs validation

  • MoGe-2 3D surfaces as a visual reference
  • Automatic landmark placement to reduce marking error
  • Correcting measurements for head angle; our first quick attempt did not work
  • iPhone TrueDepth capture: app being built, pilot next

Not ready

  • Millimetre measurements from AI 3D
  • Combining several clinic photos into one 3D face
  • Measuring from collage-sized images

Next steps, and how clinics can help

Share original photos

Full-resolution camera files, de-identified, with the same frontal and from-below views at each visit.

Add a quick 3D scan

A few seconds of 3D capture at some visits (a TrueDepth burst from our app, or 3dMD/Vectra), alongside the usual photos. Even 30–50 infants would let us validate AI measurements on real babies.

Mark landmarks

Clinician landmark markings on a set of photos let us train automatic landmark placement and measure how consistent human marking is.

Glossary

Ratio (cleft ÷ non-cleft)
A measurement on the cleft side divided by the same measurement on the other side. 1.0 means symmetric.
View from below
Basal or submental photo with the head tilted back so both nostrils face the camera.
Ground truth
The exact, known shape we compare against, available only for computer-generated and model faces.
Depth Anything 3, MoGe-2, Depth Pro, Sapiens
AI models that estimate 3D shape from ordinary photos, by ByteDance, Microsoft, Apple and Meta.
Shape error
How far the AI's nose surface is from the true surface, in mm, after allowing for a small overall shift.
Tilt error
How wrongly the AI estimates which way each part of the nose surface faces, in degrees.

Limits: computer-generated faces with synthetic skin texture; three teaching models rather than patient scans; simulated landmark marking; no infant photos with matching 3D scans yet. Nothing here is clinical validation.
